# Fe I collision and radiative level identity audit

The final published [Conroy et al. (2020), *Astrophysical Journal* **902**, 68](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abb3fd) describes a DARC electron-impact calculation with 485 bound target levels and effective collision strengths at 19 temperatures from 1,000 to 100,000 K. Its full level and collision tables are identified as `fe_i_nrg.dat` and `fe_i_coll.dat`, but the local final journal PDF contains only excerpts and has no embedded files. Neither complete table was found in the inspected local reference and atomic-data trees. The paper compares theoretical, energy-adjusted GRASP0 spontaneous rates with NIST values; it does not supply a complete machine-readable spontaneous-rate table in the inspected publication. Its Equation (2) adjusts an A value by an observed-to-calculated photon-energy ratio raised to `2k+1`, where `k` is multipole order. Thus the reported theoretical rates cannot be reconstructed from a wavelength and a generic E1 rule alone.

The local Cloudy/Stout `fe_1.nrg`, `fe_1.tp`, and `fe_1.coll` files contain 846 energy levels, 2,542 strictly positive spontaneous records representing 2,533 unique radiative pairs, and **zero electron-impact collision rows**. The nine repeated pairs have distinct multipole records, such as M1 and E2, and their A values must be added if that radiative source is selected. The six collision-rate records in `fe_1.coll` are H or proton rates only. The files identify Stout radiative data as NIST dated October 18, 2013. Their SHA-256 values, measured counts, and individual Table 2 energy residuals are in [local_inventory.json](local_inventory.json). [inspect_local_fei.py](inspect_local_fei.py) regenerates that inventory from the local files and final PDF; it never modifies the sources.

For the first 50 article Table 2 levels, all 50 Stout levels **at the same index** have the published observed energy to within 0.0527 cm^-1 after conversion from rounded Rydbergs, and all have the corresponding statistical weight. Configuration, LS term, parity, J, and energy agree for **49 of 50**. Level 27 is unresolved: Conroy Table 2 prints `3d7 4s 3P0` while Stout labels the same 20,037.816 cm^-1, J=0 state `3d6 4s2 a 3P0`. The nearby 3P levels and the existence of another `3d7 4s 3P0` Stout level show why the energy match does not establish identical configuration. Different dominant configuration labels can also reflect configuration mixing; this discrepancy alone does not prove that the observed physical state differs. The full machine-readable Conroy level table and state classification must resolve the join before assigning its collision rates. The paper itself reports substantial reordering of higher levels, especially above index 150.

Within the **first 50 Stout indices**, 106 radiative pairs have both endpoints inside the index cut. One touches unresolved level 27, leaving 105 pairs with endpoints among the 49 provisionally matched low levels. This is only an identity audit of the article excerpt, not an adopted 49-level physical atom. For the first **485 Stout indices**, 2,456 unique radiative pairs remain and 101 excited levels have no listed spontaneous decay to another level in that cut. These are *index-cut inventory counts*, not mapped Conroy-485 transition counts: the two 485-level selections cannot be equated by index. No absent A value is interpreted as physical zero.

Stout's rates also have a different provenance from Conroy's GRASP0 rates. For example, its Stout `A(1,21)=4.93e3 s^-1` is the **NIST comparison** in Conroy Table 3; the article's GRASP0 value is `5.88e3 s^-1`. Likewise, Stout `A(9,16)=4.30e-3 s^-1` is the NIST comparison, versus GRASP0 `3.17e-2 s^-1`. Some Table 3 entries cover one multipole only: Stout has both M1 `1.20e-2` and E2 `4.00e-3 s^-1` for pair (4,60), while the article's row compares its E2 value to the NIST E2 value. Combining or replacing these rows without their multipole identities would be incorrect.

A ten-level low-term calculation could use the separate OPEN-ADAS Pelan–Berrington 1997 electron source only inside its listed 100–3,980 K temperature grid, after verifying its own ten-level energies, A values, and source-use conditions. It does not cover the requested 1,000–100,000 K interval. The 485-level Conroy calculation remains a promising electron source for that interval, but a coherent cooling atom still needs (1) the complete published energy and collision tables, (2) a configuration/J/parity/energy match of every retained collision state to the proposed radiative source, and (3) radiative closure evidence for states with no listed Stout decay, or the original complete GRASP0 A table. A raw integer-index join or assumed zero rate is not supported. The current evidence supports no full-range Fe I cooling installation.

The NASA ADS API was attempted first for further metadata, but the sandbox had no DNS resolution and the one elevated retry timed out during automatic approval review. The final journal PDF was already local and read directly. The official [OPEN-ADAS Fe listing](https://open.adas.ac.uk/element/fe) was inspected; its listed Fe 0+ ADF04 entries do not identify the announced Conroy 485-level release. This does not establish that no other remote copy exists.
